<h2>Cellular antennas in Israel: permits, thresholds and published measurements</h2>
<p>According to the Non-Ionizing Radiation Law, 2006 [חוק הקרינה הבלתי מייננת, התשס״ו-2006], Israel regulates radiation sources and radiation-measurement services through permits. The law’s purpose is to protect the public and the environment while regulating the construction and operation of sources and the provision of measurement services.</p>

<h3>What the permit process establishes</h3>
<p>The law requires permits to construct or operate a regulated radiation source and to provide radiation-measurement services. Before construction, the applicant must obtain an exposure assessment from a licensed measurement-service holder. Before an operating permit is issued, measurements around the operating source must demonstrate compliance with the exposure levels set by the supervising authority.</p>
<p>The statutory text gives operating and measurement-service permits a validity of 5 years. A construction permit is valid for up to 3 months and may be extended to a maximum total validity of 9 months. For a particular antenna, inspect the actual permit and its status rather than assuming that a historic entry proves current authorisation.</p>
<p>Section 19 requires publication of source locations, permit information, exposure assessments, test results, operational measurements and construction-permit applications. These records let you move beyond the question “Is there an antenna?” to the more useful questions “What was authorised, where was exposure assessed, and what was measured?”</p>

<h3>The health threshold and the environmental threshold</h3>
<p>Israel’s environmental health report (2020), drawing on ministry information, describes broadcasting-site limits of 10% of the international health threshold where people stay for prolonged periods, including homes and offices, and 30% where people are present briefly, including roads and pavements. The international reference comes from the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection, or ICNIRP.</p>
<p>Those percentages describe exposure limits. They are not estimates of the chance of illness. ICNIRP issued updated radio-frequency guidelines in 2020, but formal Israeli adoption of those revised guidelines as the basis of its thresholds was not verified in the available evidence.</p>

<h3>What the register actually shows</h3>
<p>The published site maxima had a median of 2.95% of the health threshold and a highest value of 29.96%. No recorded site maximum exceeded 50%. The last periodic check was dated 2026 for 4,786 sites and 2025 for 1,672 sites. These dates matter when you ask whether a report describes the installation you see today.</p>
<p>The median summarises the highest reported result at each site. It is not a national average of exposure inside homes. Nor can the highest register value establish a breach of the residential threshold without the measurement location and applicable conditions. Request the underlying report before comparing a site maximum with the limit for prolonged occupancy.</p>

<h2>What the science says about radio-frequency radiation</h2>
<p>According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer, or IARC, radio-frequency electromagnetic fields were classified in 2011 as “possibly carcinogenic to humans”, Group 2B. That classification identifies a possible cancer hazard. It does not quantify your personal risk from living near an antenna or establish that a particular permitted installation causes cancer.</p>

<h3>Why the original classification needs context</h3>
<p>IARC described the evidence as limited for glioma and acoustic neuroma among wireless-phone users. Evidence concerning environmental exposure from radio, television and telecommunications transmitters was judged inadequate. These are different exposure situations. A result about using a handset cannot simply be transferred to the address of someone living near a base station.</p>
<p>The IARC announcement referred to a study reporting a 40% higher glioma risk in its heaviest phone-use category, averaging 30 minutes daily over 10 years. That was a finding from a particular study, not IARC’s estimate of the risk from antennas. It should not be used as a property-specific risk percentage.</p>

<h3>The human evidence reviews</h3>
<p>A systematic review published in 2024 with support from the World Health Organization, or WHO, found moderate-certainty evidence that mobile-phone use likely does not increase glioma, meningioma or acoustic neuroma risk. For fixed-site transmitters, including base stations, it found moderate-certainty evidence that exposure likely does not increase childhood leukaemia risk, and low-certainty evidence that it may not increase paediatric brain tumours.</p>
<p>The distinctions in certainty are part of the findings. “Moderate certainty” and “low certainty” should not be flattened into an unconditional declaration that every possible effect has been excluded. The review found no eligible studies concerning fixed-site transmitter exposure and the adult tumour outcomes it was assessing. That is an evidence gap, not a finding of increased risk.</p>
<p>The companion review published in 2025 found low-certainty evidence that mobile-phone use does not increase leukaemia, non-Hodgkin lymphoma or thyroid cancer. It also found insufficient evidence to assess whole-body far-field exposure, the category relevant to people near transmitters. Findings for handset use therefore do not resolve every question about residential exposure.</p>

<h3>Animal evidence and remaining uncertainty</h3>
<p>A WHO-supported animal review published in 2025 concluded that radio-frequency exposure increased cancer incidence in experimental animals, with the highest certainty for malignant heart schwannomas and gliomas. Its authors cautioned that extrapolation to people is particularly complex. According to the Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency, published criticisms also identified errors in that review, many of which the authors acknowledged.</p>
<p>Animal findings deserve consideration alongside human evidence. They do not provide a measured risk for your apartment, and criticism of a review does not by itself settle the underlying scientific question. The reviewed outcomes, exposure conditions and confidence in the findings all matter.</p>
<p>WHO statements cited in the evidence say that adverse health effects have not been established from base-station radio-frequency signals. Read that alongside the more recent reviews and their specific gaps. The practical conclusion is to check actual exposure and permit compliance while recognising uncertainty. Neither the presence of a mast nor a general reassurance can replace the information for your address.</p>

<h2>Power lines, substations and transformer rooms</h2>
<p>According to the Ministry of Environmental Protection’s measurement guidance and correspondence, electricity-grid magnetic fields require a different assessment from cellular radio-frequency exposure. Reports commonly express these magnetic fields in milligauss, abbreviated mG. The field changes with electrical current, so the operating conditions during a measurement matter.</p>

<h3>What the Israeli guidance means</h3>
<p>A ministry letter from 2021 describes a precautionary objective of minimising prolonged public exposure above 4 mG as a daily average on the day of peak electricity consumption. The same letter describes a 24-hour peak-day average of 4 mG as equivalent to an annual average of 2 mG. That equivalence belongs to the ministry’s assessment approach; it is not a conversion to apply automatically to a casual reading.</p>
<p>The environmental health report (2020) records joint ministry recommendations issued in 2011: 2,000 mG for momentary exposure and 4 mG for continuous, prolonged exposure. It states that these recommendations had not become a legally binding standard. A later change in their legal status was not verified. A brief reading must therefore be interpreted against the appropriate duration and guidance, rather than compared indiscriminately with either figure.</p>
<p>The ministry’s 2021 letter also states that daily average exposure in more than 90% of Israeli homes did not exceed 0.4 mG, based on studies and measurements. That background observation does not predict conditions beside a particular installation.</p>

<h3>What research found</h3>
<p>IARC classified extremely low-frequency magnetic fields as possibly carcinogenic, Group 2B, in 2001. According to WHO’s assessment, that classification rests on pooled studies showing a consistent twofold association with childhood leukaemia at average residential exposures above 0.3 to 0.4 µT, equivalent to 3 to 4 mG. WHO concluded that the evidence was not strong enough to establish causation, partly because of methodological concerns such as possible selection bias.</p>
<p>The European Union scientific committee SCHEER assessed the evidence linking these fields with childhood leukaemia as weak in its final opinion adopted in 2024. The association remains a reason for careful assessment and precaution, but it does not establish that a particular line caused an illness. The ministry’s annual-average figure of 2 mG must not be substituted for the exposure range in WHO’s description of those studies.</p>

<h3>What a property measurement can establish</h3>
<p>A documented ministry investigation examined a home 17.5 m from the axis of a 161 kV overhead line. Indoor readings were 3.3 to 7.5 mG, becoming 6.6 to 15 mG after normalisation for current. The ministry found that the results did not meet its recommendations. The investigation included monitoring for about a week. This is evidence about that home, not a universal result for every property at the same distance.</p>
<p>A transformer room houses electrical transformer equipment. Its relevance to your apartment cannot be resolved from its name or a map omission. No official universal separation distance between a transformer room and an apartment was established in the evidence. Ask where the room is relative to the occupied rooms and obtain a licensed assessment where the circumstances warrant it.</p>
<p>For lines, substations and transformer rooms, ask the surveyor to identify measurement locations, timing, operating conditions, any current normalisation and the basis for interpreting prolonged exposure. The ministry’s measurement procedure uses line-current information. A report should explain how its measurements support its conclusion, not merely provide a reassuring label.</p>

<h2>“Safe distance”: what official sources say and what they do not</h2>
<p>According to the Knesset Economics Committee’s draft planning text (2018), a public-health safety range is specific to the transmitting installation. It has horizontal and vertical dimensions related to the applicable exposure limits. The evidence did not establish a universal distance that makes every home near a cellular antenna safe or unsafe.</p>

<h3>Why the 300 m figure is often misunderstood</h3>
<p>A parliamentary draft from 2018 required an application map to identify existing or planned transmitters within 300 m. It also described a map at a scale of 1:250. The 300 m figure was a mapping radius, not a residential exclusion zone. The final adopted text was not verified, so the draft must not be represented as a confirmed current filing requirement.</p>
<p>The same draft discussed horizontal safety ranges of 4 m and, in specified combined-installation circumstances, 6 m for a rooftop permit exemption. Those figures do not establish general distances between antennas and homes. Ask for the approved exposure assessment and the safety range for the actual installation.</p>

<h3>Power-line clearances are a separate question</h3>
<p>The cited summary of a planning document reproduced by Kfar Saba municipality gives horizontal clearances from the line axis to the nearest part of an occupied building: 20 m for 161 kV and 35 m for 400 kV lines. These are planning clearances, not magnetic-field exposure limits.</p>
<p>Commercial claims of universally safe distances of 50 m and 60 m were not verified in an official primary source. Do not treat those claims, the planning clearances or the antenna mapping radius as substitutes for an exposure assessment. Ask which document supports a quoted distance, what it is measured from and what question it actually answers.</p>

<h2>Plants that report emissions: understanding the Israeli PRTR</h2>
<p>According to the Ministry of Environmental Protection, Israel’s Pollutant Release and Transfer Register, or PRTR, is published annually under the environmental reporting law of 2012. The ministry also submits a report to the Knesset Interior and Environmental Protection Committee.</p>
<p>For an English-speaking buyer, the essential distinction is between an emissions record and a measurement of the air at home. The register tells you about the reporting facility and reported substances. It does not by itself establish your personal exposure.</p>

<h3>Read the year and the substance</h3>

<p>The ministry’s register supports examination by facility and pollutant and comparisons between years. <h2>Noise: residential rules and airport operations</h2>
<p>According to the Prevention of Nuisances (Unreasonable Noise) Regulations, 1990, the assessment depends on the building category, noise duration and time of day. <h3>Residential noise limits and measurement</h3>
<p>For a home in a residential zone, the transcription gives 50 dB(A) for noise lasting more than 9 hours during the day and 40 dB(A) for noise lasting more than 30 minutes at night. <p>For tonal or impulsive noise, the rules described in the evidence add 5 dB to the measured level used in the assessment. <p>The regulations generally exclude aircraft, train, vehicle and construction-equipment noise, which have separate rules. <h2>Before you buy or rent: a practical checklist</h2>
<p>According to section 19 of the Non-Ionizing Radiation Law, exposure assessments and operational measurement results are information the ministry must publish. Begin with the records for the actual address and nearby installations. Then identify what remains unanswered before commissioning a survey or making a contractual commitment.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Identify the property precisely.</strong> Confirm the building, apartment and occupied rooms. Ask how a nearby installation relates to those rooms, rather than relying only on the address marker.</li>
<li><strong>Separate existing facilities from applications.</strong> A pending permit request describes a different situation from an operating source. Ask for the status and date of the relevant record.</li>
<li><strong>Obtain the underlying reports.</strong> For antennas, request the permit, exposure assessment and latest measurement report. For electricity infrastructure, request the relevant magnetic-field assessment.</li>
<li><strong>Check what was actually measured.</strong> Identify the location, date, units, averaging period and operating conditions. Ask whether the report supports a conclusion about the apartment.</li>
<li><strong>Follow up other layers separately.</strong> Check the reporting year for industrial emissions, the status of suspected land and the planning documents for a fuel station. Do not combine unrelated findings into a single unsupported risk rating.</li>
<li><strong>Investigate the noise you expect to live with.</strong> Ask about recurring times and obtain a professional assessment where a regulatory question remains unresolved.</li>
<li><strong>Put the outstanding questions in writing.</strong> Ask the appropriate professional to identify what is established, what is missing and whether a further document or measurement could resolve it.</li>

<h2>Your rights: permits, TAMA 36 and compensation claims</h2>
<p>According to the Non-Ionizing Radiation Law, residents can obtain published permit and measurement information, and the legislation provides routes for challenging certain supervisory decisions. Planning objections, radiation enforcement and compensation claims are different processes. Before taking action, identify the decision you want challenged and the authority responsible for it.</p>

<h3>The Israeli terms in plain English</h3>
<p><strong>TAMA:</strong> a national outline plan that sets planning provisions for a particular subject.</p>
<p><strong>TAMA 36/A:</strong> the antenna-related national outline plan cited in the planning decision described in the evidence.</p>
<p><strong>Local planning committee:</strong> the local planning body from which you can request the relevant application and planning file.</p>
<p><strong>Section 197:</strong> the Planning and Building Law compensation provision referenced in claims about property-value loss associated with a plan.</p>
<p><strong>Indemnity letter:</strong> an undertaking concerning compensation liability required in the cited antenna-permit material.</p>

<h3>What an objection can and cannot establish</h3>
<p>In a Tel Aviv licensing decision from 2020, a neighbour’s objection was rejected because the antenna application complied with TAMA 36/A. The authority stated that radiation was not a planning argument in that case and that radiation matters were not its responsibility. That decision must not be expanded into a claim that every objection is impossible or that every antenna automatically receives permission.</p>
<p>Ask the committee which procedure applies to the particular application and whether an objection route is available. A planning lawyer can distinguish a planning argument from a concern that should be raised with the radiation regulator. The evidence does not establish a universal objection deadline or a guaranteed outcome.</p>
<p>The cited permit decision required an indemnity letter concerning section 197 claims. That requirement does not establish that neighbouring owners will receive compensation. Successful claims and typical compensation amounts for antenna-related loss of value were not verified.</p>
<p>Section 21 of the radiation law allows a person affected by a supervisory instruction or order to ask a court to cancel it. Filing does not suspend the order unless the court decides otherwise. The legislation also extended environmental-nuisance civil claims to non-ionizing radiation. Which route fits your circumstances requires legal assessment of the actual documents and alleged harm.</p>

<h3>How do I check air pollution in an Israeli city?</h3>
<p>Identify the relevant Ministry of Environmental Protection monitoring records, then request results for the pollutants and periods that concern you. <h3>What is the PRTR?</h3>
<p>It is Israel’s Pollutant Release and Transfer Register, an annual record of reported emissions and waste transfers from large plants. Read each entry by reporting year, pollutant and release or transfer category. A nearby reporting facility does not by itself establish exposure at your home.</p>

<h3>Does a suspected contaminated-land entry mean the property is contaminated?</h3>
<p>No. Suspected and confirmed are different statuses. A suspected entry also does not prove that investigation has cleared the site. Ask for the underlying record and any subsequent findings, then have an environmental consultant explain what they establish about the property.</p>

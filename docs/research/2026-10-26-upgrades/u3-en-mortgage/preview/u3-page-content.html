Israel Mortgage for Foreigners and Non-Residents: What You Can Borrow

An Israel mortgage for foreigners is capped at 50% of the home's value: Bank of Israel Directive 329 treats any buyer without Israeli citizenship as buying an investment home. Israeli citizens living abroad and registered new olim can borrow up to 75% for a first Israeli home. Average fixed rate: 4.62% (August 2026).

In Hebrew the loan is a mashkanta. Two limits apply and the lower one wins: the cap for your status, and a monthly payment of no more than 50% of your disposable income. A ₪2,000,000 loan over 25 years at the August 2026 average fixed rate costs ₪11,253 a month (≈ $3,616). On a ₪4,000,000 home, a buyer without Israeli citizenship needs about ₪2,344,610 in cash to close, including the 8% purchase tax a foreign resident pays (≈ $753,409; dollar figures approx., Bank of Israel rate of 23 Oct 2026).

How much an Israeli bank can lend you, by status

Israeli banks do lend to buyers who live abroad. What surprises most of them is how the loan is built: the cap depends on your citizenship, not on where you live; part of the loan may be linked to the consumer price index; at least a third has to sit on a fixed rate; you need an Israeli bank account before the money is paid out; and life insurance assigned to the bank is a condition.

Bank of Israel Directive 329 sets three caps on the loan as a share of the home's value: 75% for a single home, 70% for a replacement home and 50% for an investment home. The first two are defined only for an individual Israeli citizen. A single home is the buyer's only home in Israel and the Area, and the household counts as one buyer: you, your spouse unless you live permanently apart, and your children under 18 unless they are married.

The Bank of Israel's own questions and answers on the directive closes the question for foreigners: because single and replacement homes are defined only for an Israeli citizen, a home bought by a foreign resident "is necessarily an investment home". That is why a buyer without Israeli citizenship gets 50%, even for a first and only home here.

"Israeli citizen" in the directive is a legal term borrowed from section 16A of the Land Taxation Law. It covers anyone registered, or required to be registered, in the Population Registry; an individual who is an Israeli resident; and a person entitled to make aliyah under the Law of Return who lives in the Area. The Bank of Israel applies these words as written and declines to interpret them, so on a borderline case the bank's answer is the one that counts. Ask for it in writing.

The value the bank lends against is the lower of the contract price and its own appraisal. If the appraiser values the home below the price you agreed, the loan shrinks and the gap comes out of your cash, even though the seller still expects the full price. Check the financing before you sign, not after the payment schedule is binding. For a buyer abroad the gap is sharper, because the extra money has to pass source of funds checks when it arrives from abroad.

Banks say the same in their own words. Mizrahi-Tefahot's English questions and answers say olim get up to 75% instead of the 50% they would get as foreign citizens, if they are Israeli citizens or entitled to Israeli citizenship. The directive's own test is the definition above, so treat "entitled" as a question for the bank, not a promise. For olim and diaspora buyers, status planning is part of mortgage planning, not only a tax question.

Two rulebooks: citizenship sets your loan, residency sets your tax

Two Israeli authorities look at you through different windows. The Bank of Israel sizes your loan by citizenship. The Tax Authority sizes your purchase tax by residency. The two tests do not have to agree, and for Israelis who live abroad they usually do not.

Take an Israeli citizen who lives in London and buys a first home in Tel Aviv for ₪4,000,000. For the bank he is an Israeli citizen buying a single home, so he can borrow 75%, ₪3,000,000. For the Tax Authority he is not an Israeli resident, so he pays the foreign resident rate, 8% up to ₪6,055,070 and 10% above, from the first shekel: ₪320,000 on this home. A buyer without citizenship pays the same ₪320,000 but can borrow only ₪2,000,000. A new oleh who lives in Israel can borrow ₪3,000,000 and, under the oleh relief, pays ₪10,106.

The foreign resident rate of 8% and 10% is a temporary order, extended to purchases until 10 February 2027 inclusive. According to the Tax Authority's 2026 guide for buyers and sellers, a foreign resident who becomes an Israeli resident within two years of buying can be treated as a resident for the single home test and claim the difference back. Tax filing has its own clock: the declaration goes in within 30 days of the deal, and the tax falls due 60 days from it. The purchase tax itself is covered in our guide to purchase tax for foreign residents.

Do not confuse the loan cap with the tax. The bank may treat you as an investor while the Tax Authority treats you as a single home buyer, or the other way round. Both land on the same budget, so work out the loan, your own money and the purchase tax together before you commit to a price.

The income test: 50% by law, about one third in practice, in your currency

The second limit is your income. Directive 329 caps the monthly payment at 50% of disposable income, which is net income minus fixed commitments that will run for more than 18 months, such as other loans. Above 40%, the directive makes the bank set more capital aside for the loan, so many banks stop well below the legal cap. Discount Bank's English page says most banks offer up to about one third. In August 2026 the average new mortgage in Israel took 29.23% of the borrowers' income.

The payment is in shekels, so the income behind it is counted in shekels too. If you earn in dollars, euros or pounds, what you need changes with the exchange rate. And the test is not the whole decision: banks also look at your assets, how stable your income is and your age at the end of the loan, each under its own credit policy.

Mortgage rates in Israel today, by track and by term

Israeli mortgage rates are read through three numbers: the Bank of Israel rate, the prime rate built on it, and the rate each track of your loan carries. The Bank of Israel rate is 3.25%, in force since 3 September 2026 after the third cut since May, and it was left unchanged in October. Prime is the Bank of Israel rate plus 1.5, so 4.75%. Inflation over the 12 months to September 2026 was 1.6% (Central Bureau of Statistics). The next rate decision is on 23 November 2026; in its October 2026 forecast the Bank of Israel expects its rate to average 3.0% in the third quarter of 2027 and inflation of 2.0% over the next four quarters.

The Bank of Israel also publishes the average rate on new housing loans every month. The latest month available on 25 October 2026 is August 2026. These are averages across all borrowers in Israel, not an offer for your file, but they are the best yardstick for any offer you get.

Read the CPI-linked rates with care. A CPI-linked track adds inflation to the balance, so its lower rate does not compare with a not linked rate. On the Bank of Israel's 2.0% inflation forecast, a 3.29% linked rate costs about 5.4% a year in shekels, more than the 4.62% fixed not linked average (our arithmetic, August 2026 rates).

This is the linkage trap. On a CPI-linked track the inflation adjustment is added to the principal, so the balance can rise even while every payment is made on time. A buyer who looks only at the first monthly payment understates the cost, and the risk is larger for someone who thinks in dollars, euros, pounds, Canadian or Australian dollars but repays a shekel loan.

A second Bank of Israel table gives average rates by the length of the loan (not linked and CPI-linked). Banks use it, among other things, to work out early repayment fees. The figures are effective annual averages for the reporting month of September 2026, in force from 19 October 2026.

Use these figures to test an offer, not to replace one. Ask each bank for a written breakdown by track: which part can change with prime, which part resets later, which part is linked to the index, whether the balance can rise, and what early repayment would cost. The lowest opening payment is not necessarily the lowest risk loan.

Tracks and the one-third fixed rule

An Israeli mortgage is usually built from several tracks rather than one loan at one rate. The mix matters more than the headline rate, because each track reacts to something different: prime, the consumer price index, a reset date, or an exchange rate. A fixed not linked track gives the most clarity; a fixed CPI-linked track can start lower but its balance follows inflation; a prime track moves with every rate decision; a track that resets every 5 years looks calm at the start and can change later; a foreign currency track can match foreign income but adds exchange and benchmark risk.

Directive 329 limits the variable part to 66.66% of the loan, so at least one third must sit on a fixed rate. The rule leaves room for many mixes, which is why Directive 451 makes every approval in principle show three uniform baskets the Bank of Israel defined: all fixed not linked; thirds of fixed not linked, prime and variable CPI-linked every 5 years; and half fixed not linked, half prime. Each basket comes with its expected total interest, total repayment and highest expected monthly payment, so you can compare banks on the same terms (the Bank of Israel explains this in English).

The right mix depends on the currency you earn in, how much payment risk you can carry, how long you plan to hold the home, whether you may repay early and whether aliyah is on the horizon. This guide gives no ready-made mix, because no single mix suits every foreign buyer. What it can tell you is how each track behaves, and that is what to ask the bank to show you in writing.

Borrowing in dollars, euros or pounds

Israeli banks can lend in foreign currency or linked to it, but few borrowers take it. In August 2026 foreign currency loans were 0.1% of new housing lending by volume, all of them on a variable rate; no fixed foreign currency loans were made that month. Their average rate was 4.86%: the foreign benchmark at 2.93% plus an average margin of 1.92.

For a non-citizen one rule makes them more flexible. Directive 329 section 12.3 lets a bank leave foreign currency housing loans to a foreign resident out of the two-thirds variable cap, provided all its variable housing loans approved in principle in that quarter stay within 66.66%. In plain words, the bank may allow a fully variable foreign currency loan if its overall book allows it. A bank that rarely gives housing loans need not meet even that test for a foreign resident client.

A foreign currency loan suits you only if you earn in the same currency and expect to keep doing so. The apartment, the purchase tax and most costs stay in shekels, so the loan also moves the shekel value of your debt. Bank of Jerusalem is known for foreign residents' business, but none of the banks' English pages we read on 25 October 2026 listed current foreign currency loan terms. Ask in writing which currencies are offered, at what margin and for how long.

The 10% shekel test

If you borrow in shekels and earn abroad, run the payment through a 10% move in the exchange rate. A ₪2,000,000 loan over 25 years at the August 2026 average of 4.62% costs ₪11,253 a month, ≈ $3,616 at the Bank of Israel rate of 23 Oct 2026. If the shekel weakens 10% ($1 = ₪3.4232), the same payment costs you ≈ $3,287; if it strengthens 10% ($1 = ₪2.8008), ≈ $4,018. Over the 30 days to 23 October 2026 the dollar alone moved from ₪3.017 to ₪3.112.

The price bracket: above ₪6,055,070 the foreign resident rate rises to 10% on the part above it. And the date: the 8% and 10% rate applies to purchases until 10 February 2027.

The price is in shekels. Under the Consumer Protection Law (section 17D) a dealer may publish the price of a home only in Israeli currency, so read the dollar, euro and pound figures as conversions at one day's rate: they will move.

Documents, the Israeli bank account and identification from abroad

A mortgage from abroad is a paperwork project. The bank has to identify you, test your income, trace the money you bring, value the home and meet its own compliance duties. A clean file moves; a weak one gets delayed, sent back for translations or approved for less.

Two sets of rules sit behind the requests. The money laundering order for banks says no account is opened without identification and a know-your-customer process: where the money comes from, what you do, why you need the account and how you plan to use it. For a foreign resident it adds two questions: your connection to Israel, and whether you are a foreign public figure. Directive 451 then makes the bank give you the list of documents it needs for the loan together with the approval in principle, so you get one list, in writing.

A checklist to start the file

  • Passport and identity documents for every borrower, plus a second photo ID.
  • Israeli identity or citizenship documents, if you have them or may be entitled to them.
  • Proof of your address outside Israel.
  • Tax returns from the country where you live.
  • Payslips, an employer letter or an accountant's letter showing current income.
  • Bank and asset statements showing your own money and reserves.
  • Documents showing where the money for your own part comes from.
  • The draft or signed purchase contract, depending on the stage.
  • Property documents the bank, your lawyer or the appraiser ask for.
  • Translations, where the bank requires them.
  • Life insurance assigned to the bank.
  • Property insurance the bank requires.
  • A power of attorney, if you will sign through a lawyer or a representative.

Income earned abroad needs more paper than an Israeli salary. A salaried buyer may need an employer letter and income history; a self-employed buyer, accountant letters and tax filings; a company owner, the company's accounts. Mizrahi-Tefahot says it counts income from Israel or abroad, including investment income. The exact package depends on the bank, the country and your profile, so confirm it before you sign a binding contract.

The money you bring for your own part is checked separately from the loan. The bank may ask where it came from, when it was earned, how it moved between accounts and whether it is yours. Plan the transfer route early, so the money is not stuck in review when a payment date arrives.

The Israeli bank account

In practice you need an Israeli account before the loan is paid out, because the payments run through it. Open it early: the opening triggers its own know-your-customer review, and a contract with a tight payment date becomes stressful if the account, the identity checks or the incoming transfer are still under review.

From approval in principle to the money: the timeline

The order of the steps protects you. Get the approval in principle before you sign, match the contract's payment dates to the dates the bank can release money, and leave room for the appraisal and the insurance. A contract that assumes fast funding puts you under pressure if any step slips.

There is no official figure for how long the whole process takes, so treat any promise in days or weeks as an estimate. The two clocks set by rule are the bank's answer on the approval in principle and its answer on an outside insurance policy.

Buying a new apartment from a developer

A new home bought from a developer adds timing to the loan. You pay in stages, and the bank releases the loan according to its own rules and the project's. It will want to see the project, the developer's documents, the payment schedule and the security you get for your payments. Compare new projects in Israel, or browse the English catalog of new building projects, once your loan ceiling and the route for your own money are clear.

Before you sign anything, ask the bank or a mortgage broker for a written pre-check: the most it will lend, the expected track mix, how the appraisal could change the loan, the document list and how you will sign from abroad.

Signing from abroad and insurance

Much of the process can be done without flying to Israel, but each bank decides which signatures it accepts. The usual routes are a power of attorney to an Israeli lawyer, signatures before an Israeli consul, or a local notary with an apostille. Some banks still want an in-person or consular signature. Confirm the route with the bank before you sign the purchase contract or book anything.

Israeli missions abroad notarize signatures in person, before a consular representative; they do not prepare legal documents for you. The service is also open to a foreign citizen who needs a notarized signature on a document to be submitted in Israel, so bring the document ready to sign.

Find out early whether the bank will want you in person at any stage, whether it needs original documents, and whether opening the account requires a branch visit. At Bank of Jerusalem, for example, a non-resident account is opened at a branch and not by proxy.

Life and property insurance

Banks require life insurance and property insurance assigned to them as a condition of the loan. You do not have to buy them from the bank: Directive 451 lets you bring an outside policy, and the bank must confirm it received it and tell you and the insurer within 14 days if the policy does not meet its requirements. For older buyers or buyers with a medical history, start the insurance early, because a delayed or limited approval can hold up the loan.

Olim and returning Israelis

Aliyah changes the loan before it changes anything else. Once you are registered in the Population Registry you count as an Israeli citizen under Directive 329, and a first home can be financed up to 75% instead of 50%. Returning Israelis who stayed in the Registry are Israeli citizens for the bank wherever they have lived.

Timing matters. Some brokers' and news pages say buyers who plan aliyah within two years can already get 75%. The directive's text does not say so, so if your plan depends on it, get the bank's answer in writing before you sign. The same applies to a buyer who is entitled to make aliyah but has not yet done so.

On the tax side, an oleh buying a single home from one year before aliyah to seven years after it can use the oleh relief of regulation 12A: 0% up to ₪1,978,745, 0.5% up to ₪6,055,070 and 8% up to ₪20,183,565, with no relief above that. On a ₪4,000,000 home that is ₪10,106. A buyer who becomes an Israeli resident within two years after buying as a foreign resident can be treated as a resident for the single home test and claim a refund.

Olim may also be eligible for a state-assisted mortgage with an eligibility certificate, the teudat zakaut. We do not print its amount or rate here: the figures quoted on bank and broker pages differ, and we found no official 2026 figure on 25 October 2026. Ask the bank for the current terms in writing.

The oleh certificate also opens a bank account: the Bank of Israel told banks it counts as an ID card for up to 30 days from its issue. Status improves the cap, but it does not replace the credit decision; the bank still looks at your income, the home, your ability to repay, the insurance and the documents.

Above 50%: what exists and what it costs

No Israeli bank can lend a buyer without Israeli citizenship more than 50% of the value of a home, because Directive 329 binds every bank. Any route above 50% is therefore outside the bank.

Brokers advertise extra loans from non-bank lenders on top of the bank mortgage. Those lenders are not banks, the directive does not apply to them, and their terms appear in no official comparison. If you consider one, ask for the full cost in writing: the rate, every fee, the term, the security they take, and what happens to the bank mortgage if a payment is missed.

Israelis who sell one home to buy another sometimes use a bridge loan between the two deals. For an Israeli citizen, a home bought to replace one he will sell is financed up to 70%. A non-citizen stays at 50% whatever he sells.

The plain way above 50% is more of your own money, a less expensive home, or a change of status that the bank accepts in writing.

What Israeli banks say on their own English pages

The large Israeli mortgage lenders active with foreign buyers include Mizrahi-Tefahot, Bank Leumi, Bank Hapoalim, Israel Discount Bank and Bank of Jerusalem. The lender's name matters, but so does the desk that handles your file: not every branch or banker is used to foreign income.

Leumi, Hapoalim and Discount are major banks with broad mortgage activity. For you the question is not whether a bank has a mortgage department, but whether it will understand foreign income, accept translated documents, open the account, handle the know-your-customer questions and keep to the contract's timetable. Some bank pages give 40% or one third as their own payment limit; that is their policy, and the directive's ceiling stays 50%.

Going direct or through a mortgage broker

You can go to the banks yourself, and many foreign buyers use a broker who works with non-residents. A good broker does more than shop for a rate: he packages foreign income in a form an Israeli bank can underwrite, knows which bank is likely to understand your file, explains the track mixes and keeps the documents moving.

Going direct works when your income is a simple salary, your documents are strong, your Israeli status is clear and you have time to speak with several banks; it also shows you how each bank communicates. A broker earns his fee when you are self-employed, earn in several currencies, own a company, hold complex assets, want a foreign currency loan or will sign through a power of attorney. Neither route guarantees approval.

A broker does not replace your lawyer. The broker handles the loan; the lawyer handles the rights in the property, the contract, the purchase tax filing, the warning note, the registration and the legal risk. The two should talk before you sign if the payment schedule depends on the loan.

Whichever way you go, ask for written answers: the most the bank will lend, how the appraisal affects it, the documents, the steps to open the account, the insurance, how you will sign, the track mix, the index exposure and when the money will be released. A vague answer on financing becomes a cash problem very quickly.

Frequently asked questions about Israeli mortgages for foreigners

Can a foreigner get a mortgage in Israel?

Yes. Israeli banks lend to buyers without Israeli citizenship, mostly in shekels, up to 50% of the home's value. Bank of Israel Directive 329 treats any home a non-citizen buys as an investment home, so the 50% cap applies even to your first and only home in Israel. The value is the lower of the price and the bank's appraisal, and your payment must also pass the income test.

How much will an Israeli bank lend me?

Two limits apply and the lower one wins. The first is the cap by status under Directive 329: 50% for a non-citizen, 75% for an Israeli citizen's only home, 70% for a replacement home. The second is the income cap: the monthly payment may not pass 50% of your disposable income, and most banks prefer about one third. Banks count the value as the lower of price and appraisal.

What is the minimum down payment?

For a non-citizen, half the price plus purchase tax and fees. A home priced at ₪933,600 (≈ $300,000, approx., Bank of Israel rate of 23 Oct 2026) needs ₪466,800 down from a non-citizen, plus ₪74,688 purchase tax at 8%. An Israeli citizen buying an only home needs 25%, ₪233,400, plus the purchase tax of his residency track: 8% if he lives abroad, nothing at this price if he lives in Israel.

I am an Israeli citizen living abroad. Can I borrow 75%?

Yes, for your only home in Israel. The directive's "Israeli citizen" includes anyone registered, or required to be registered, in the Population Registry, wherever you live today. Your purchase tax is a different matter: for the Tax Authority you are not an Israeli resident, so you pay the foreign resident rate of 8% up to ₪6,055,070 and 10% above, for purchases until 10 Feb 2027.

I am making aliyah. When does 75% start?

When you count as an Israeli citizen under the directive, which in practice means once you are registered. Mizrahi-Tefahot's English page, for example, offers olim 75% instead of 50%. Some brokers' and news pages say buyers planning aliyah within two years can get 75% already; the directive's text does not say so. If your budget depends on it, get the bank's answer in writing before you sign.

My spouse is Israeli and I am not. What can we borrow?

The Bank of Israel follows the Tax Authority here. If you are separate buyers for tax, the cap is the average weighted by your shares: half each of 75% and 50% gives 62.5%. But spouses normally count as one buyer for the single home test, so how your household is treated is a question for the bank, in writing, before you sign.

What are mortgage rates in Israel now?

The latest Bank of Israel averages on new loans are for August 2026: fixed not linked 4.62%, fixed CPI-linked 3.29% plus inflation, variable CPI-linked 3.19% plus inflation, and foreign currency loans 4.86%. Prime is 4.75%, the Bank of Israel rate of 3.25% (since 3 Sep 2026) plus 1.5. The next rate decision is on 23 Nov 2026. Your own quote depends on the bank, the track mix, the loan share, your income and the term.

Do foreigners pay higher rates than Israelis?

The Bank of Israel does not publish rates by citizenship, so there is no official figure either way. What changes for a foreign buyer is the size of the loan, the paperwork and the time it takes. Compare the written offers you receive against the official monthly averages, track by track.

Which bank in Israel is best for foreigners?

There is no official ranking. Mizrahi-Tefahot runs an International Mortgage Center (shekel loans, up to 50%, up to 30 years); Discount Bank and Bank of Jerusalem have English pages for clients abroad, and Leumi and Hapoalim also lend to foreign buyers. The right bank depends on your status, your income country, your currency and the banker's experience with files like yours. The Bank of Israel publishes rate comparisons by bank.

Can I borrow in dollars, euros or pounds?

Yes, Israeli banks can lend in foreign currency or linked to it. The average rate on those loans was 4.86% in August 2026, and they were 0.1% of new housing lending. For a non-citizen they can sit outside the two-thirds variable cap, if the bank's quarterly book allows it. They suit you only if you earn in that currency.

Should I borrow at home instead?

Compare the total cost in the currency you earn. A shekel loan moves with the exchange rate: a ₪2,000,000 loan over 25 years at the August 2026 average of 4.62% costs ₪11,253 a month, ≈ $3,616 at the Bank of Israel rate of 23 Oct 2026, and ≈ $3,287 or ≈ $4,018 if the shekel weakens or strengthens 10%. A loan at home avoids that swing but follows your home country's rules.

How much do I need to earn?

The payment may not pass 50% of your disposable income, and most banks prefer about one third. A ₪2,000,000 loan over 25 years at the August 2026 average of 4.62% costs ₪11,253 a month, so you need ₪22,507 net a month at the legal cap (≈ $7,232) and ₪33,760 at one third (≈ $10,848), approx., Bank of Israel rate of 23 Oct 2026.

How do banks count income earned abroad?

Each bank sets its own policy. The directive counts net income minus fixed commitments that run for more than 18 months. Expect translated payslips or tax returns, an accountant's letter if you are self-employed, and source of funds checks on the money you bring. Mizrahi-Tefahot, for example, says it counts income from Israel or abroad, including investment income.

What documents do I need?

A passport and a second photo ID, proof of income, bank statements, evidence of where your own money comes from, the contract and property papers, life and property insurance, and signatures or a power of attorney. Under Directive 451 the bank must give you its list of documents together with the approval in principle, so ask for it then.

Do I need an Israeli bank account, and can I open one as a non-resident?

In practice yes: the loan is paid out and repaid through it. A bank may not unreasonably refuse a shekel current account that stays in credit. You can be identified face to face abroad by an Israeli lawyer or an Israeli consul, but each bank decides its own process; Bank of Jerusalem, for example, opens non-resident accounts only at a branch and not by proxy.

How long does approval take?

The bank must answer a request for approval in principle in writing within 5 business days, or 7 in cases its credit policy defines in advance. The appraisal, the insurance and the security come after that, and there is no official figure for the whole process, so treat any promise in weeks as an estimate.

Can I do it all without flying to Israel?

Often, yes: with a power of attorney to an Israeli lawyer, and with signatures before an Israeli consul or a notary with an apostille. Some banks still want an in-person or consular signature, or a branch visit to open the account. Each bank decides which signatures it accepts, so confirm before you sign the purchase contract or book anything.

Is life insurance required, and can I use my own policy?

Yes. Banks require life and property insurance assigned to them as a condition of the loan. You may bring an outside policy instead of the bank's: under Directive 451 the bank must tell you and the insurer within 14 days if the policy does not meet its requirements. Older buyers should start the insurance early.

What is the longest mortgage term?

30 years under Bank of Israel Directive 329, for any buyer. Within that limit, age limits and the term a bank will actually offer you are each bank's own policy. For comparison, the average new not linked loan in Israel in August 2026 ran for 23.8 years.

Does part of the loan have to be fixed?

Yes. The variable part may not pass 66.66% of the loan, so at least one third sits on a fixed rate, linked or not. Prime and tracks that reset every 5 years count as variable. The exception is a foreign currency loan to a non-citizen, which a bank may leave outside the cap if its quarterly book stays within it.

What if the appraisal comes in below the price?

The bank lends on the lower figure and you cover the gap in cash. On a ₪4,000,000 home valued at ₪3,800,000, a non-citizen's loan falls from ₪2,000,000 to ₪1,900,000. A report by an appraiser on the bank's list can be used for 90 days, so time the appraisal close to the contract.

How much purchase tax does a foreign buyer pay?

8% up to ₪6,055,070 and 10% on the part above, from the first shekel, for purchases until 10 Feb 2027 inclusive. On ₪3,000,000 that is ₪240,000; on ₪4,000,000, ₪320,000. The test is residency, not citizenship: an Israeli citizen living abroad pays the same rate.

Can I repay early, and what does it cost?

Prime and other tracks whose rate changes at least once a year carry no capitalization fee. Fixed tracks can, when market rates have fallen below your rate; the fee drops by 20% after 3 years and 30% after 5. There is also an operational fee of up to ₪60, and 0.1% if you give less than 10 days' notice.

Can I borrow more than 50% as a foreigner?

Not from an Israeli bank while you are not an Israeli citizen: Directive 329 binds every bank. Brokers offer extra loans from non-bank lenders on top of the mortgage; those lenders work outside the directive. Ask for the full cost in writing, including the rate, every fee and the security they take.

What is the monthly payment on a $500,000 loan over 30 years?

$500,000 is ₪1,556,000 at the Bank of Israel rate of 23 Oct 2026. At the August 2026 average fixed not linked rate of 4.62%, a 30-year loan of that size costs ₪7,995 a month, about $2,569. As a non-citizen you would need a home worth at least ₪3,112,000 to borrow that much.

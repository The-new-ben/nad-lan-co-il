Our honesty statement: what this page shows and what it does not

  • Every figure is published. Every map layer comes from an open government register (the Ministry of Environmental Protection, the Ministry of Energy) or from OpenStreetMap, with its source and the date we read it.
  • Distance, not exposure. The map measures distance. It does not calculate radiation, magnetic fields, noise or pollution inside a flat. Only a licensed surveyor measures that.
  • There is no official "safe distance". No official source we found sets a safe distance from a cellular antenna or a power line. If someone quotes such a number, ask where it comes from.
  • No mark does not mean no hazard. Transformer rooms, fuel and gas pipelines, airport noise contours and live air-pollution values are not published as open data, so they are not on the map.
  • No names. We do not show the names of mobile operators, plants or site owners next to a hazard.
  • "Suspected" stays suspected. Most sites in the national soil database are marked as suspected contamination that was not confirmed, and they are marked that way here.

Cellular antennas in Israel: permits, thresholds and published measurements

According to the Non-Ionizing Radiation Law, 2006 [חוק הקרינה הבלתי מייננת, התשס״ו-2006], Israel regulates radiation sources and radiation-measurement services through permits. The law’s purpose is to protect the public and the environment while regulating the construction and operation of sources and the provision of measurement services.

What the permit process establishes

The law requires permits to construct or operate a regulated radiation source and to provide radiation-measurement services. Before construction, the applicant must obtain an exposure assessment from a licensed measurement-service holder. Before an operating permit is issued, measurements around the operating source must demonstrate compliance with the exposure levels set by the supervising authority.

The statutory text gives operating and measurement-service permits a validity of 5 years. A construction permit is valid for up to 3 months and may be extended to a maximum total validity of 9 months. For a particular antenna, inspect the actual permit and its status rather than assuming that a historic entry proves current authorisation.

Section 19 requires publication of source locations, permit information, exposure assessments, test results, operational measurements and construction-permit applications. These records let you move beyond the question “Is there an antenna?” to the more useful questions “What was authorised, where was exposure assessed, and what was measured?”

The health threshold and the environmental threshold

Israel’s environmental health report (2020), drawing on ministry information, describes broadcasting-site limits of 10% of the international health threshold where people stay for prolonged periods, including homes and offices, and 30% where people are present briefly, including roads and pavements. The international reference comes from the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection, or ICNIRP.

Those percentages describe exposure limits. They are not estimates of the chance of illness. ICNIRP issued updated radio-frequency guidelines in 2020, but formal Israeli adoption of those revised guidelines as the basis of its thresholds was not verified in the available evidence.

What the register actually shows

The published site maxima had a median of 2.95% of the health threshold and a highest value of 29.96%. No recorded site maximum exceeded 50%. The last periodic check was dated 2026 for 4,786 sites and 2025 for 1,672 sites. These dates matter when you ask whether a report describes the installation you see today.

The median summarises the highest reported result at each site. It is not a national average of exposure inside homes. Nor can the highest register value establish a breach of the residential threshold without the measurement location and applicable conditions. Request the underlying report before comparing a site maximum with the limit for prolonged occupancy.

What the science says about radio-frequency radiation

According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer, or IARC, radio-frequency electromagnetic fields were classified in 2011 as “possibly carcinogenic to humans”, Group 2B. That classification identifies a possible cancer hazard. It does not quantify your personal risk from living near an antenna or establish that a particular permitted installation causes cancer.

Why the original classification needs context

IARC described the evidence as limited for glioma and acoustic neuroma among wireless-phone users. Evidence concerning environmental exposure from radio, television and telecommunications transmitters was judged inadequate. These are different exposure situations. A result about using a handset cannot simply be transferred to the address of someone living near a base station.

The IARC announcement referred to a study reporting a 40% higher glioma risk in its heaviest phone-use category, averaging 30 minutes daily over 10 years. That was a finding from a particular study, not IARC’s estimate of the risk from antennas. It should not be used as a property-specific risk percentage.

The human evidence reviews

A systematic review published in 2024 with support from the World Health Organization, or WHO, found moderate-certainty evidence that mobile-phone use likely does not increase glioma, meningioma or acoustic neuroma risk. For fixed-site transmitters, including base stations, it found moderate-certainty evidence that exposure likely does not increase childhood leukaemia risk, and low-certainty evidence that it may not increase paediatric brain tumours.

The distinctions in certainty are part of the findings. “Moderate certainty” and “low certainty” should not be flattened into an unconditional declaration that every possible effect has been excluded. The review found no eligible studies concerning fixed-site transmitter exposure and the adult tumour outcomes it was assessing. That is an evidence gap, not a finding of increased risk.

The companion review published in 2025 found low-certainty evidence that mobile-phone use does not increase leukaemia, non-Hodgkin lymphoma or thyroid cancer. It also found insufficient evidence to assess whole-body far-field exposure, the category relevant to people near transmitters. Findings for handset use therefore do not resolve every question about residential exposure.

Animal evidence and remaining uncertainty

A WHO-supported animal review published in 2025 concluded that radio-frequency exposure increased cancer incidence in experimental animals, with the highest certainty for malignant heart schwannomas and gliomas. Its authors cautioned that extrapolation to people is particularly complex. According to the Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency, published criticisms also identified errors in that review, many of which the authors acknowledged.

Animal findings deserve consideration alongside human evidence. They do not provide a measured risk for your apartment, and criticism of a review does not by itself settle the underlying scientific question. The reviewed outcomes, exposure conditions and confidence in the findings all matter.

WHO statements cited in the evidence say that adverse health effects have not been established from base-station radio-frequency signals. Read that alongside the more recent reviews and their specific gaps. The practical conclusion is to check actual exposure and permit compliance while recognising uncertainty. Neither the presence of a mast nor a general reassurance can replace the information for your address.

Power lines, substations and transformer rooms

According to the Ministry of Environmental Protection’s measurement guidance and correspondence, electricity-grid magnetic fields require a different assessment from cellular radio-frequency exposure. Reports commonly express these magnetic fields in milligauss, abbreviated mG. The field changes with electrical current, so the operating conditions during a measurement matter.

What the Israeli guidance means

A ministry letter from 2021 describes a precautionary objective of minimising prolonged public exposure above 4 mG as a daily average on the day of peak electricity consumption. The same letter describes a 24-hour peak-day average of 4 mG as equivalent to an annual average of 2 mG. That equivalence belongs to the ministry’s assessment approach; it is not a conversion to apply automatically to a casual reading.

The environmental health report (2020) records joint ministry recommendations issued in 2011: 2,000 mG for momentary exposure and 4 mG for continuous, prolonged exposure. It states that these recommendations had not become a legally binding standard. A later change in their legal status was not verified. A brief reading must therefore be interpreted against the appropriate duration and guidance, rather than compared indiscriminately with either figure.

The ministry’s 2021 letter also states that daily average exposure in more than 90% of Israeli homes did not exceed 0.4 mG, based on studies and measurements. That background observation does not predict conditions beside a particular installation.

What research found

IARC classified extremely low-frequency magnetic fields as possibly carcinogenic, Group 2B, in 2001. According to WHO’s assessment, that classification rests on pooled studies showing a consistent twofold association with childhood leukaemia at average residential exposures above 0.3 to 0.4 µT, equivalent to 3 to 4 mG. WHO concluded that the evidence was not strong enough to establish causation, partly because of methodological concerns such as possible selection bias.

The European Union scientific committee SCHEER assessed the evidence linking these fields with childhood leukaemia as weak in its final opinion adopted in 2024. The association remains a reason for careful assessment and precaution, but it does not establish that a particular line caused an illness. The ministry’s annual-average figure of 2 mG must not be substituted for the exposure range in WHO’s description of those studies.

What a property measurement can establish

A documented ministry investigation examined a home 17.5 m from the axis of a 161 kV overhead line. Indoor readings were 3.3 to 7.5 mG, becoming 6.6 to 15 mG after normalisation for current. The ministry found that the results did not meet its recommendations. The investigation included monitoring for about a week. This is evidence about that home, not a universal result for every property at the same distance.

A transformer room houses electrical transformer equipment. Its relevance to your apartment cannot be resolved from its name or a map omission. No official universal separation distance between a transformer room and an apartment was established in the evidence. Ask where the room is relative to the occupied rooms and obtain a licensed assessment where the circumstances warrant it.

For lines, substations and transformer rooms, ask the surveyor to identify measurement locations, timing, operating conditions, any current normalisation and the basis for interpreting prolonged exposure. The ministry’s measurement procedure uses line-current information. A report should explain how its measurements support its conclusion, not merely provide a reassuring label.

“Safe distance”: what official sources say and what they do not

According to the Knesset Economics Committee’s draft planning text (2018), a public-health safety range is specific to the transmitting installation. It has horizontal and vertical dimensions related to the applicable exposure limits. The evidence did not establish a universal distance that makes every home near a cellular antenna safe or unsafe.

Why the 300 m figure is often misunderstood

A parliamentary draft from 2018 required an application map to identify existing or planned transmitters within 300 m. It also described a map at a scale of 1:250. The 300 m figure was a mapping radius, not a residential exclusion zone. The final adopted text was not verified, so the draft must not be represented as a confirmed current filing requirement.

The same draft discussed horizontal safety ranges of 4 m and, in specified combined-installation circumstances, 6 m for a rooftop permit exemption. Those figures do not establish general distances between antennas and homes. Ask for the approved exposure assessment and the safety range for the actual installation.

Power-line clearances are a separate question

The cited summary of a planning document reproduced by Kfar Saba municipality gives horizontal clearances from the line axis to the nearest part of an occupied building: 20 m for 161 kV and 35 m for 400 kV lines. These are planning clearances, not magnetic-field exposure limits.

Commercial claims of universally safe distances of 50 m and 60 m were not verified in an official primary source. Do not treat those claims, the planning clearances or the antenna mapping radius as substitutes for an exposure assessment. Ask which document supports a quoted distance, what it is measured from and what question it actually answers.

Air pollution: Israeli values, WHO guidelines and monitoring stations

According to the Clean Air Law, 2008, section 6 provides the framework for air-quality values. Target values express policy goals. Ambient values, also called environmental values in Israeli material, are the binding category. Alert values concern immediate-risk conditions. These categories should remain separate when you read a report about an Israeli city.

What a nearby monitoring station tells you

The Ministry of Environmental Protection published locations for 159 monitoring stations in a list last updated in April 2024. Live readings were not available in an open-data form usable for this map. A station location therefore shows where monitoring is recorded, not the current concentration at your prospective home.

Request the relevant monitoring results, pollutant names, dates and averaging periods. Ask whether the information supports a conclusion about the address or describes a wider area. A brief observation during a viewing cannot establish annual air quality. Air-pollution reports can be directed through the environmental hotline *6911 or the government call centre 1299.

Plants that report emissions: understanding the Israeli PRTR

According to the Ministry of Environmental Protection, Israel’s Pollutant Release and Transfer Register, or PRTR, is published annually under the environmental reporting law of 2012. It records reported releases to air, sea, land and water sources, together with waste transfers from large plants. The ministry also submits a report to the Knesset Interior and Environmental Protection Committee.

For an English-speaking buyer, the essential distinction is between an emissions record and a measurement of the air at home. The register tells you about the reporting facility and reported substances. It does not by itself establish your personal exposure.

Read the year and the substance

The snapshot checked on 3 November 2026 contained 801 plants recorded since 2012. The 2025 release reports covered 566. These figures describe different reporting populations. A historical listing must not be mistaken for confirmation that a facility appears in the latest annual report.

The ministry’s register supports examination by facility and pollutant and comparisons between years. When reading a record, identify the reporting year, the substance, the destination of the release or transfer and the relevant unit. Do not treat releases to air and waste transfers as the same kind of information, or infer conditions inside an apartment from proximity alone.

The State Comptroller has identified Haifa Bay as a prominent environmental pollution hotspot, and the annual emissions report includes a dedicated section on the area. However, the government decision concerning relocation of petrochemical industry and its dates were not verified in the evidence. A property decision should not assume that a nearby activity will end on an unconfirmed timetable.

Fuel stations, contaminated land and other infrastructure

According to the Ministry of Energy register, 1,255 fuel stations were recorded in the snapshot checked on 3 November 2026. According to the Ministry of Environmental Protection’s contaminated-land database, there were 3,829 sites, including 3,614 recorded as suspected rather than confirmed. These entries call for different follow-up questions.

Fuel-station distances were not verified

Secondary reporting about a 2016 change to TAMA 18, the national outline plan concerning fuel stations, describes a distance of 40 m from housing for a station with 4 fuelling positions. It also describes possible reductions to 20 m in urban-renewal plans. A legal-summary source mentions 80 m between a station and public buildings under amendment 4.

The binding plan text was not verified. These figures must not be treated as confirmed universal rules for your property or as proof of safety. The cited material also records Ministry of Health concerns about carcinogenic substances such as benzene when distances were shortened. It does not supply an exposure result for any particular neighbouring apartment.

Ask the local planning authority which plan and conditions govern the specific station and neighbouring plot. Have a suitable professional explain any environmental documents before treating the map distance as decisive.

Suspected contamination is a status, not a diagnosis

A suspected-land entry does not confirm that contamination has been found. Equally, its suspected status does not establish that the site has been investigated and cleared. Request the underlying record, the reason for the status, any investigation results and any document changing that status. Ask an environmental consultant what those records establish about the property you are considering.

The available evidence does not provide universal residential setbacks for pipelines or a complete map of transformer rooms. It also does not establish property-specific conclusions about radon, flooding or seismic hazards. Those missing layers should remain unanswered questions where relevant, rather than becoming assumptions that no issue exists.

Noise: residential rules and airport operations

According to the Prevention of Nuisances (Unreasonable Noise) Regulations, 1990, the assessment depends on the building category, noise duration and time of day. The cited transcription defines day as 06:00 to 22:00 and night as 22:00 to 06:00. A noise number without those conditions cannot establish whether the relevant limit has been exceeded.

Residential noise limits and measurement

For a home in a residential zone, the transcription gives 50 dB(A) for noise lasting more than 9 hours during the day and 40 dB(A) for noise lasting more than 30 minutes at night. Its original table layout was not verified against the official text. These figures are therefore a starting point for professional checking, not a complete legal assessment.

For tonal or impulsive noise, the rules described in the evidence add 5 dB to the measured level used in the assessment. Measurements are taken inside a habitable room, at least 1 m from walls and 1.2 to 1.5 m above the floor, with windows and external doors open and internal doors closed. An informal reading taken in different conditions cannot simply be compared with the regulatory table.

The regulations generally exclude aircraft, train, vehicle and construction-equipment noise, which have separate rules. They include specified exceptions, such as car parks for 25 or more vehicles and construction equipment operating permanently at a site. Ask an acoustic consultant which rules apply to the source you actually hear.

Ben Gurion airport at night

The cited aircraft-noise summary reports a general take-off restriction from 01:40 to 05:50. It separately describes a take-off closure from 02:00 to 05:00, with exceptions for Thursday nights and nights before holiday eves. Landings are not restricted to fixed hours. These reported periods do not amount to a promise of uninterrupted silence.

The precise current operating provisions were not verified against the primary text. According to the cited monitoring information, the Ministry of Environmental Protection and the Israel Airports Authority monitor aircraft noise continuously, and monitoring reports are published. Residents can approach the environmental information centre responsible for the airport or the ministry’s district office.

TAMA 2/4, the airport’s national outline plan approved in 1997, describes aircraft-noise zones and insulation conditions. The State Comptroller reported in 2016 that the operating pattern in the plan was outdated and recommended an update. The available evidence does not establish the current contour affecting a particular address.

Make your concern specific

Record when the noise occurs, how long it lasts, which rooms are affected and whether it recurs. Ask for the applicable measurement method and a written interpretation. When comparing homes, arrange a visit during the period that concerns you, and distinguish your comfort preferences from a claim that a legal threshold has been breached.

Before you buy or rent: a practical checklist

According to section 19 of the Non-Ionizing Radiation Law, exposure assessments and operational measurement results are information the ministry must publish. Begin with the records for the actual address and nearby installations. Then identify what remains unanswered before commissioning a survey or making a contractual commitment.

  1. Identify the property precisely. Confirm the building, apartment and occupied rooms. Ask how a nearby installation relates to those rooms, rather than relying only on the address marker.
  2. Separate existing facilities from applications. A pending permit request describes a different situation from an operating source. Ask for the status and date of the relevant record.
  3. Obtain the underlying reports. For antennas, request the permit, exposure assessment and latest measurement report. For electricity infrastructure, request the relevant magnetic-field assessment.
  4. Check what was actually measured. Identify the location, date, units, averaging period and operating conditions. Ask whether the report supports a conclusion about the apartment.
  5. Follow up other layers separately. Check the reporting year for industrial emissions, the status of suspected land and the planning documents for a fuel station. Do not combine unrelated findings into a single unsupported risk rating.
  6. Investigate the noise you expect to live with. Ask about recurring times and obtain a professional assessment where a regulatory question remains unresolved.
  7. Put the outstanding questions in writing. Ask the appropriate professional to identify what is established, what is missing and whether a further document or measurement could resolve it.

What to ask when the documents are in Hebrew

You can request an English explanation without asking someone to reduce a technical report to “safe” or “unsafe”. Useful questions include:

  • “Please identify the permit number, issuing authority, validity and current status.”
  • “Does this percentage refer to the health threshold or the environmental threshold, and where was it measured?”
  • “Was this measurement taken inside the apartment, and what occupancy period does the comparison assume?”
  • “Does the magnetic-field report adjust for electrical current or assess prolonged exposure?”
  • “Does this land record say suspected contamination, confirmed contamination or something else?”
  • “Which parts of your conclusion come from official documents, and which still need confirmation?”

Keep the original Hebrew documents alongside any English explanation. Ask the surveyor or lawyer to identify the passages supporting their conclusion. For an overseas purchase, give the person inspecting the property these questions in advance so that the resulting report addresses your decision.

Your rights: permits, TAMA 36 and compensation claims

According to the Non-Ionizing Radiation Law, residents can obtain published permit and measurement information, and the legislation provides routes for challenging certain supervisory decisions. Planning objections, radiation enforcement and compensation claims are different processes. Before taking action, identify the decision you want challenged and the authority responsible for it.

The Israeli terms in plain English

TAMA: a national outline plan that sets planning provisions for a particular subject.

TAMA 36/A: the antenna-related national outline plan cited in the planning decision described in the evidence.

Local planning committee: the local planning body from which you can request the relevant application and planning file.

Section 197: the Planning and Building Law compensation provision referenced in claims about property-value loss associated with a plan.

Indemnity letter: an undertaking concerning compensation liability required in the cited antenna-permit material.

What an objection can and cannot establish

In a Tel Aviv licensing decision from 2020, a neighbour’s objection was rejected because the antenna application complied with TAMA 36/A. The authority stated that radiation was not a planning argument in that case and that radiation matters were not its responsibility. That decision must not be expanded into a claim that every objection is impossible or that every antenna automatically receives permission.

Ask the committee which procedure applies to the particular application and whether an objection route is available. A planning lawyer can distinguish a planning argument from a concern that should be raised with the radiation regulator. The evidence does not establish a universal objection deadline or a guaranteed outcome.

The cited permit decision required an indemnity letter concerning section 197 claims. That requirement does not establish that neighbouring owners will receive compensation. Successful claims and typical compensation amounts for antenna-related loss of value were not verified.

Section 21 of the radiation law allows a person affected by a supervisory instruction or order to ask a court to cancel it. Filing does not suspend the order unless the court decides otherwise. The legislation also extended environmental-nuisance civil claims to non-ionizing radiation. Which route fits your circumstances requires legal assessment of the actual documents and alleged harm.

Frequently asked questions

Are cellular antennas dangerous?

According to the WHO-supported human review published in 2024, fixed-site exposure likely does not increase childhood leukaemia risk, while evidence for other outcomes is less certain or incomplete. IARC’s Group 2B classification remains relevant context. Check the actual permit and exposure measurements rather than deciding from the presence of an antenna alone.

What is a safe distance from a cell tower in Israel?

No universal residential distance was established in the evidence. Antenna safety ranges are specific to the installation and have horizontal and vertical dimensions. The frequently mentioned 300 m figure concerns mapping in draft application requirements, not a general safety boundary around homes.

How do I find out if there is an antenna near an apartment?

Start with the address map, then check the Ministry of Environmental Protection’s source and permit records. Distinguish active sites from pending applications. Request the exposure assessment and latest measurement report for any installation you want to understand more closely.

How much radiation was measured at antennas in Israel?

In the register snapshot checked on 3 November 2026, the median of the highest reported results at active sites was 2.95% of the health threshold. The highest was 29.96%. These are site-report statistics, not measurements inside every nearby apartment or percentages of health risk.

How much does a radiation test cost in Israel?

The cited commercial figures from 2026 give NIS 500 to 650 for a standard apartment. The service usually includes radio-frequency and electrical magnetic-field measurements. Confirm the licensed surveyor’s scope, written report, any extended monitoring and VAT before booking.

Are high-voltage power lines dangerous?

Research has found an association between higher average residential magnetic fields and childhood leukaemia, but causation has not been established. Israel’s published guidance takes a precautionary approach. Assess the magnetic field in the occupied rooms and the relevant duration, rather than relying solely on distance.

What is a transformer room and is it dangerous?

It is a room containing electrical transformer equipment. Its presence does not establish the magnetic-field exposure inside a neighbouring apartment. No universal official separation distance was verified. Where it is relevant to the property, ask a licensed surveyor to assess exposure in the occupied rooms.

How do I check air pollution in an Israeli city?

Identify the relevant Ministry of Environmental Protection monitoring records, then request results for the pollutants and periods that concern you. The map’s station markers are locations, not live readings. Keep annual values, shorter-period results, legal ambient values and WHO guidelines separate.

What is the PRTR?

It is Israel’s Pollutant Release and Transfer Register, an annual record of reported emissions and waste transfers from large plants. Read each entry by reporting year, pollutant and release or transfer category. A nearby reporting facility does not by itself establish exposure at your home.

Can I object to a new antenna near my home?

Ask the local planning committee which procedure applies and whether an objection route is available. Planning questions and radiation-regulation questions may require different approaches. The cited Tel Aviv decision rejected a particular objection; it does not establish the outcome of every application.

Is a fuel station near a home dangerous?

A nearby station warrants examination of its applicable planning and environmental documents. The evidence mentions concerns about benzene, but provides no exposure result for your address. Reported setback distances were not checked against the binding plan and should not be treated as safety guarantees.

What noise level is allowed at night in Israel?

The cited transcription gives 40 dB(A) for noise exceeding 30 minutes at night in a residential-zone home. The original table layout was not verified. Building category, source, duration and measurement conditions matter, and aircraft or transport noise follows separate provisions.

Should I test for radiation before buying an apartment in Israel?

A licensed survey can help where nearby infrastructure, missing reports or unclear measurements leave a material question about the apartment. Begin by examining existing records and ask what an additional survey would resolve. The evidence does not establish that every purchase requires a new test.

Where can I see the official Israeli data in English?

This page explains the mapped records in English, but a complete official English version of all relevant registers and documents was not verified. Request the original ministry or planning records and an English explanation of the relevant passages. Keep that explanation with the source documents.

Does a suspected contaminated-land entry mean the property is contaminated?

No. Suspected and confirmed are different statuses. A suspected entry also does not prove that investigation has cleared the site. Ask for the underlying record and any subsequent findings, then have an environmental consultant explain what they establish about the property.

Does a clear map mean I can skip further checks?

No. The map has gaps, including transformer rooms and several other environmental layers. Use it to identify available records and the questions they leave unanswered. Whether further work is useful depends on the property and the documents already available.
